How it works

An autostereogram hides a depth map by repeating a strip of pattern across each row and shrinking the repeat distance wherever the hidden surface comes closer. Every point on the hidden surface links two pixels, x − s/2 and x + s/2, that were given the same colour, where s is the local repeat. Solving the picture means recovering s at every pixel, then turning it into brightness (near is bright by default).

This is an inverse problem without a unique answer: many shifts match a repeating texture, s and s + period both fit in flat regions, and the sign of depth depends on whether the picture was made for parallel or cross-eyed viewing. The solver picks the answer that minimises an energy

E(s) = Σp C(p, sp) + Σneighbours P1·[|sp − sq| = 1] + P2·[|sp − sq| > 1]

where C is the truncated, window-averaged difference between the two half-shifted copies of the image at shift s (the data term), and the P1/P2 terms are a smoothness prior that lets depth change gently for a small price and pays a fixed price for a real edge. The background period is found first from the self-similarity curve, the shift range is limited to a band around it (removing the period ambiguity), and the energy is minimised approximately with semi-global matching: dynamic programming along eight directions, summed, then a per-pixel winner with sub-pixel refinement.
